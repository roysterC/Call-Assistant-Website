# Pilot salon: plan for the first case study

Made with the **customer-research** skill (primary research: go and ask).
Goal: in about four weeks, end with **one real result, one real quote and written permission to use them.** Everything else in marketing gets stronger once these exist.

Nothing here names the founder or the pilot salon. The case study names the salon only if the owner agrees.

## 1. Agree the basics first (week 0)
Put these in a short email so nobody has to remember them:
- **Length:** four weeks, with a check-in each week.
- **Cost:** **MISSING.** Free, discounted, or normal price after the pilot? Decide before you ask.
- **What it does and doesn't do:** answers and books calls, replies to messages, sends confirmations and reminders. It never transfers calls; it takes messages instead.
- **Their data:** the salon's client list stays the salon's. If they stop, it's exported back to them and deleted.
- **Permission:** say upfront that you'd like to ask, at the end, whether you can share results and a quote. They can say no.

## 2. Measure "before" (the most important step)
Without a before, there's no after. Before switching Kikai on, collect:
- **Missed calls:** from the salon phone's call log, or the phone provider's online account, count unanswered calls for the last two weeks. Note when they happened (during appointments, lunch, after closing).
- **No-shows:** count them in the diary for the last four weeks.
- **Interruptions:** for one normal week, a tally sheet by the phone: one mark each time someone stops a service to answer.

## 3. Set it up
- Price list, team, who does what, opening hours, house rules and skin-test policy go into Kikai.
- Move their clients and upcoming appointments across.
- **Forwarding:** an easy start is to forward only unanswered calls (after a few rings, or when the line is busy). Staff still pick up when they're free, and Kikai catches the rest. Switch to forwarding every call once they trust it.

## 4. During the pilot (weeks 1 to 4)
From the Kikai app, each week:
- Call History: calls answered, how many outside opening hours, and how each ended (booked, left a message, enquiry).
- Bookings Kikai made, and the value of those services from the price list.
- Callbacks taken, and texts sent.
- No-shows in the diary.

Ten-minute check-in each week: "Did it get anything wrong?" Write down every mistake and fix it fast. A case study is only believable if the problems were real and handled.

## 5. The conversation at the end (week 4)
Twenty minutes over a coffee. Keep it casual: don't call it research or an interview, so the answers are real rather than polite. Write down their exact words.

Ask, then follow each answer with "why does that matter?" two or three times:
1. Walk me through a busy Saturday now, compared with before.
2. Tell me about a call it handled that surprised you.
3. Was there a time it got something wrong? What happened next?
4. How would you feel if you couldn't use Kikai any more: very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, or not disappointed?
5. What would make you stop using it?
6. If you told another salon owner about it, what would you say?
7. Who else should we talk to?

Question 5 is there to prove us wrong: the skill's first rule is to look for what breaks our assumptions, not what confirms them. Question 6 is where the quote usually comes from. Question 7 is your next pilot.

## 6. Turn it into a case study
Ask in writing, separately for each item:
- the quote, with their name and salon name, or anonymously ("a hair salon owner in the Midlands")
- a photo of the salon
- the numbers

Then write one line of result using real figures only. For example: "[N] calls answered in four weeks, [N] of them after closing, [N] bookings." Put it alongside the quote.

**Where it goes:** the website (the quote goes in `reviews` in `src/content/site.ts`; the section that shows it still needs building), a review-style ad, the leave-behind sheet, and the pinned post on Instagram.

**Keep it honest:** one salon is a strong story but a weak statistic. Say "at one salon", never "salons see".
